title: I have a problem.
description: On ambition, ownership, and making room for the things worth betting on.
date: 2026-09-22
---
I have a problem.

My problem is not tied to motivation or laziness or bad habits or even AI, rather it is tied directly to how I’ve built my life. While trying to fit so many worthwhile things into each week, I’ve built my life to be optimized for throughput rather than ownership and that has led to the only way the system can possibly be closed mathematically being by compressing the intellectual work with AI. Then because I’m not doing enough of the intellectual activity myself, all my classes have started feeling hollow and because they feel hollow, I end up looking elsewhere (advanced graduate classes, astrophysics research, SpaceX, networking, hardware projects, cycling, etc.) for experiences that make me feel genuinely alive. And those additions make my calendar even denser, which makes me outsource even more of the basic fundamental work.

And that, I think, is the problem. That is the loop I am in.

And in the Aristotelian framework I’ve been developing as I read the *Nicomachean Ethics*, it is almost the exact opposite of eudaimonia. I find myself more and more accumulating the outputs associated with an excellent life while outsourcing the activity through which I would actually become excellent. I came to this realization (and needed to write down my thoughts) by looking at my calendar for this week. Today is almost a perfect case study: I had Contemporary Civilization, lunch with friends, Physics, a Calc homework sprint, Calc, Networks for AI, and an extracurricular before meeting a friend. That’s essentially a 10am-12am chain with nowhere in there for two hours of slowly wrestling with multivariable calculus until I understand it, so of course Calc becomes “get this submitted before class.”

Tomorrow is even more revealing: I have a four-hour training session, need to study for my calculus exam, have a CC response due, Data Structures, Physics recitation, and a large coding assignment due at 11:55pm. I rode a century ride yesterday, and whatever one thinks about the physical side, purely as a time-allocation problem I’ve given four prime daytime hours to training on the same day a programming assignment is due. That virtually guarantees that Data Structures becomes an evening production sprint rather than a learning process. Then Thursday is CC --> Physics --> Calc --> Calc team test deadline --> ML info session. Friday has Astrophysics --> CSI --> an industry event. Sunday already has most of the day blocked for backlog that I didn’t have time to finish last week! So then when I think back to how I justify to myself using AI in schoolwork and what started this whole reflection: “I genuinely don’t have time to actually do work,” I find it hard not to believe my own justification. Under the current specifications of my life, I actually don’t. But I genuinely don’t know if I can sustainably operate in an even denser productivity system that squeezes another 15% out of me while also not wanting to give up on my ambitions and undertakings.

As I search the internet for students sharing similar feelings about their life and how they’ve come to deal with it, I came across a benchmark from Harvard’s 2026 senior survey: the median respondent reported about 40hrs/week on academics alongside 20hrs of social life and 15 hours of extracurricular activity; median sleep was seven hours. That’s self-reported data from one graduating class, not a prescription for me, but it demonstrates the basic arithmetic: serious academics already consume something approximating a full-time job before you add serious sport, recruiting, clubs, startups, and New York. For context, the data I collected on myself over the past month is as follows: 34.2hrs/week on academics alongside 12hrs/week of training, 13hrs of social/leisure, 10.8hrs of extracurricular, 12hrs of walking/transportation, 14hrs of life admin and roughly 7.3hrs of sleep/night. Worth noting here is that I’m not spending an extraordinarily different amount of time on school than the average Harvard student, but I am spending a very large amount of time across three competing domains simultaneously: school, endurance sports, and career/building/networking.

So then, I think to turn towards the “greats” of time-management and extraordinary achievement. And honestly, this was an interesting result because going through the biographies of these people it doesn’t seem like they balanced everything. Steve Jobs didn’t become the perfect Reed student while simultaneously pursuing everything else because once he decided to drop out, he stopped taking required courses that didn’t interest him and dropped into courses he actually cared about (the famous example being calligraphy). His retrospective lesson was that following genuine curiosity created dots that only connected later for him. Bill Gates feels almost freakishly relevant to me because according to Harvard records he had this bizarre habit of not attending courses he was registered for while auditing courses he wasn’t registered for because he found them interesting (something I strongly did in my second semester of college, and am now doing in a slightly more formal way by registering for graduate classes and zoning out in my required classes). But for Bill Gates, when the Altair opportunity appeared, he and Paul Allen spent almost eight weeks obsessively writing BASIC instead of preparing for exams. Worth noting is that years later, Gates said that he regretted not being more sociable at Harvard because he had focused so heavily on work and code (something I identified in myself after my first year of college and am actively trying to work on this year). So then Gates’ path during school seems to be evidence of the opposite of “you can do everything if you optimize enough” and instead he made enormous asymmetrical bets on whatever captured him (though later identifying the costs to that choice).

Larry Ellison told the Smithsonian that he never took a computer science course, left college, picked up a book and taught himself programming. (Interestingly enough, in the same interview he explicitly said a college degree is useful and that he would recommend getting one…). Again there’s this pattern: not “perfectly balance university, entrepreneurship, technical mastery, sport, networking, and social life” but narrow brutally. Musk’s court records from the Tesla compensation litigation show another possibility; they describe him spending roughly 80-90 hours/week primarily between Tesla and SpaceX in 2017, but when asked how he manages multiple companies he has explicitly described context switching as painful and said he tries to concentrate a given day primarily on one company. In another interview, he estimated roughly 80% of his time at Tesla/SpaceX went into engineering and design. It’s interesting because he isn’t trying to fix this context-switching issue by fracturing every hour into six unrelated projects but rather tries to group attention around a domain (though there are difficulties in exercising this “deadline freedom” in the context of college deadline culture). And then there’s Bezos who has a completely different direction than the mythology: he publicly says he protects eight hours of sleep and puts all mentally demanding meetings before lunch, and thinks his job is to make a small number of high-quality decisions rather than maximize sheer count of decisions.

So the pattern I see in these examples is obsessive selection and willingness to leave other potentially valuable things undone. Which I’m afraid might be what I’m currently resisting. So then, given what I want (something in the realm of a deep understanding in Physics & CS, SpaceX-style projects, eventually building companies, robotics/hardware, AI infrastructure, serious physical adventures and continuously challenging myself), I don’t know if I should make this semester a “fit as many interesting Columbia opportunities into my life as possible” semester. But then if I want to make this the semester where I become a genuinely capable technical builder instead of merely looking like one, I’m going to need to sacrifice a lot.

Here’s a basic idea of the hierarchy I think I would need to set up:

1. Data Structures, Physics and Calc III become real learning because I can’t keep outsourcing the substrate of these subjects and then expect myself to be excellent at AI systems, robotics, aerospace or technical startups later. And the advice I got from ex-SpaceX engineers points the same way: fundamentals, technical clarity, requirements/design/testing and projects with measurable outcomes matter in life.
2. Keep the intellectual things in which I actually come alive. I recognize this in myself in CC sometimes, Networks for AI does that, Astrophysics doesn’t currently do it, but I am relatively confident it will soon. Those then aren’t wastes just because they don’t maximize degree efficiency; they may be some of the most Aristotelian things on my calendar precisely because I’m genuinely exercising myself while doing them.
3. Robotics/hardware needs to replace something if I add it into my calendar. The goal here is to give myself a serious three-hour block a week to build with the goal that, by December, there is a system/object/program on my desk that didn’t exist in September and that I understand end-to-end.
4. Keep cycling, friendship, adventure, and New York. I don’t think the answer is to turn myself into a monk in my room (which is what I sort of did last year). My century was one of the clearest examples of doing an intrinsically meaningful activity.
5. Events/networking should become opt-in and not default. If attending this will not create something I genuinely value that I cannot reasonably get otherwise I shouldn’t attend. Because although they are great individually, collectively they are eating away the hours in which I could be building an actual skill.

The Harvard culture I described earlier has generated recent student commentary explicitly criticizing life as an endless optimization problem (overflowing calendars, every moment has to be made productive, fulfillment becoming another thing to optimize, etc.) and although it’s just an opinion piece it’s pretty close to what I’m doing as I optimize my life away. And Columbia students have also written about essentially the same impulse (enormous to-do lists, internships, professors, homework, research, and that any unused time means failing to exploit Columbia).

So then do I have enough time? I mean we all have the same 24hrs. But I don’t think I have too many interests, I just have too many interests that I promote to the status of weekly obligation. And I think I lost the point of having many interests: that you have a rich life to choose from. Doesn’t necessarily mean I need to simultaneously execute on all of them. And that funnily enough brings me back to Aristotle where right now, an uncomfortable amount of my week is structured around external goods (GPA, completed assignments, resume signals, credits, events attended, people met, internship positioning, etc.) while the stuff that I feel I get genuine happiness out of is almost entirely activity (riding the century, actually understanding Aristotle, connecting all the dots in physics, building something that finally works, being with someone I actually care about) and that’s maybe why my current life looks so good from the outside and still feels wrong from my side.

So I think I will ask myself more if I am doing something because the thing itself constitutes part of the life I want or if I am doing it primarily to acquire evidence that I am the kind of person I want to be. And honestly it is scary to allow myself to do that because I constantly feel like the second category is necessary (I need a degree and I need to submit assignments on time and I need to make money to survive) and Aristotle isn’t going to magically abolish this from my life. But I think that I need to prevent *most* of my week from becoming category two.

So then how do I move forward? I think that my working thesis for the end of this year could then be something along the lines of: learn deeply enough to become technically capable, build something that is actually mine, keep doing physically difficult and adventurous things because I genuinely love them, stay close to people I care about and treat grades, credentials, networking and optimization as constraints. Assuming I stick to this idea, then I should measure myself at Christmas against whether I can solve problems I couldn’t solve in September, whether I can build things I couldn’t build, whether there is at least one thing I made that I understand deeply, whether I became physically stronger or more capable, whether I had experiences I’ll actually remember and cherish and whether I became a more interesting, capable, courageous person?

If yes then that’s a very different year than the one I’m currently heading towards. But isn’t that what everybody is measuring themselves against in college? This feels like just the general baseline and perhaps I am completely misidentifying my fear. Because after rereading everything I just wrote, I think what I’m really afraid of is trading my ambition for manageability (in an effort to feel like I have control over my life) and then waking up in two years as a perfectly respectable Columbia student with nothing genuinely exceptional to show for it. Is that not a rational fear in a world where advanced automation is driving social stratification on a scale never before seen? This is truly what is mentally blocking me from optimizing for a “healthy balance” and shifting into a more manageable path. And perhaps this is all stemming from one unaddressed source of emotions in my recent past.

When I was building TheNetwork Labs I had an obvious identity and asymmetry, I was the college student building a company. If it worked the upside was enormous and so even when I was sacrificing school for it, there was a clear rational reason. Then I stepped back from the company, and suddenly the sacrifice remained visible despite the potential disappearing: I wasn’t as academically dialed-in as the people who had optimized entirely for school since day one and the thing that justified that sacrifice for me was gone. So retrospectively it feels like I came out of that grind as a worse student with no company, I had lost. But now with almost a semester of retrospect and many hours to reflect on it, I think I ran an experiment and yes, it did fail, but the knowledge, network, judgement, taste and tolerance for uncertainty absolutely didn’t vanish just because I stopped my work with the company.

And here I think lies the biggest lesson I have been avoiding like the plague ever since February: taking a large asymmetric bet only works if you actually take the bet. I cannot keep continuously paying the costs of ten possible bets without ever concentrating enough resources to make one of them consequential. F\*CK.

Okay so now that I’ve finally put that in writing what’s next? Honestly, I don’t think I should optimize to be a great student because having the objective of becoming excellent at Calc III, Mechanics, Data Structures, doing all the readings properly, sleeping eight hours, having friends and building little projects on Saturdays is just going to turn me into a very competent, very conventional Columbia student. So then let’s explore a metaphor.

Imagine a barbell. On the one side is a hard floor: don’t become incompetent in the fundamentals, don’t fail classes, don’t let your GPA implode, don’t reach junior year unable to code without an LLM or unable to manipulate basic mechanics/calculus; the goal here is to preserve option value. On the other side is a ceilingless bet: something should be allowed to become unreasonable.

And I think this second half is the huge missing part of the hierarchy I outlined earlier. Doing three hours of robotics every Sunday won’t get me anywhere, so I should find something I cannot stop thinking about, and for the next six weeks it should get 20hrs a week. I skip all the marginal life events and try to get absurdly deep into that idea. This sounds much closer to the Gates/Musk behavior I was pointing at earlier, or at least my understanding of it.

So then, why haven’t I felt this compulsion since TheNetwork Labs last September? I don’t know for sure, but my best bet would be that (a) I clearly can’t manufacture obsession administratively, and (b) right now I don’t have a startup I’m dying to build or a robotics problem consuming me and although Networks is interesting, it’s not yet *the thing*. So I’m in an awkward phase between bets and it seems that, in chasing the feeling I had last year, I’ve filled the vacuum with many medium-sized bets. Which is understandable in retrospect but also feels very weird because I’ve been trying to pretend I’ve found my next obsession, each time switching context between each “obsession,” creating immense mental pain and making it hard to organize my life properly.

But then how should I move forward? Certainly not through a Las Vegas Algorithm approach… I would get it eventually but who knows how long it would take and time is the finite resource in this case.

So then, we’ve established that my job right now may genuinely be *search* and this is what my process looks like right now: touch many things —> convert all of them into recurring commitments —> have very little spare capacity when one deserves escalation. Looks like we’ve found a bottleneck. If I want to remain maximally opportunistic I may need to build some slack into my life not necessarily for virtuous relaxation but because if somebody tomorrow said: “I’m building X, this is fucking insane, come spend the next month with us,” I genuinely would have no capacity to say yes without my whole life catching fire (a situation that I now realize has happened at least thrice in the past month), so yeah, not a great life strategy.

Then what is the solution? What do I optimize for?

Let’s list what we ideally preserve:

- Columbia degree and prerequisites at a strong-enough level
- Real CS/Physics fundamentals so I’m not bluffing
- Networks and high-information exploration that genuinely expands my frontier
- Serious athletics because that is genuinely part of the person I want to be
- Enough social life that I don’t isolate myself

Okay, now that we have this list, on top of that we want to build optionality and strike capacity, so that could take the shape of a deliberately protected 10-15 hour weekly strike reserve that is *not* pre-assigned? And that could be robotics this week, or a hackathon next week or a startup prototype for three weeks or a rabbit-hole-type delve into a topic and if I ever encounter something that passes the threshold from “oh that’s interesting” to “holy shit this could be revolutionary” I need to remember to not just add it on top of the current pile. Instead, to maintain the balance I need to promote it, BUT that means something else gets demoted and I become temporarily unbalanced.

I think a core reason for my intense struggle with all this is that I score myself a lot against whether I am a great student or a great founder or a millionaire or a great athlete or a great role model, etc. and in an effort to be all of those at once, I create projections of all these to the outside while inside struggling to see myself as such and that makes every exploratory period in my life feel like failure to me. But I mean I’m only 19, which means there are certainly going to be periods in which I am between things and quite honestly I haven’t even had time to compound long enough for any outputs to look impressive.

So I need to stop demanding continuous visible exceptionalism or I will be heavily incentivized to keep accumulating titles and labels just so there is never a blank space in the story, which for lack of better wording, is how I will end up LARPing permanently. So then perhaps the genuinely ambitious move is to look unimpressive for a while. And the version of me that existed a few months ago might have found this idea ridiculous because surely there’s a version of me excelling in all these domains I’m pursuing today.

But if it’s clear that to spend six months becoming extraordinarily good at X and have very little legible to show for month four is the unassuming path towards being able to suddenly do things almost nobody around me can, then shouldn’t that be the conversion I care about? And yet I still feel unsure that I will achieve happiness that way (in the Aristotelian sense of the word).

To start to address this gap in my convincing of myself, it may be useful to explore how I benchmark myself (i.e. how I create the standards I uphold myself to). In an effort to articulate it into words, I think it goes something like this: I compare myself against an imaginary composite peer. That person in my head is better than me academically, building a startup, already rich, technically excellent, getting elite internships, socially successful, athletic, well-connected, doing research, and exploring Columbia perfectly. They are ahead of me in every single one of those dimensions.

And I think that my ceiling for each of those standards is externally sourced from individual people I meet who are ahead of me in each one of those dimensions. But I need to understand that that doesn’t mean there is one peer doing all of them. So then I see myself as deficient on the scale of the maximum value observed across my entire social graph for every variable. The goal is to dominate every axis. But should it be?

It’s funny because this brings me back to a piece of advice I got from an admission officer back when I was applying to Stanford: the goal ought to be to develop a profile so unusually spiky that the combination becomes hard to replicate. And I already seem to have some ingredients for a weird combination: serious endurance, physics, CS, AI systems, entrepreneurship mindset, unusually aggressive networking and a willingness to pursue things outside the normal curriculum, but I don’t think I have figured out what all of that eventually converges into. Because the end state I want certainly isn’t “Tristan did more extracurriculars than everyone” (how abominable would that be), the more valuable end state would be “why the fuck does this guy know how to do that?!?” I think this means I need to revise my philosophy again.

To do fewer things and become everything or to do everything and optimize harder, that is the question. Or is there a third option? To keep the floor high, search aggressively, preserve unused capacity, and when something truly catches, concentrate violently while accepting being merely average at peripheral things while becoming freakishly good at the thing that matters. Then repeat. (Capacity, peripheral and optimization, maybe my Networks class is helpful after all haha).

I think the conclusion here is that being exceptional requires tolerating non-exception in some dimensions. Musk isn’t trying to be a concert pianist, professional cyclist, historian, surgeon and theoretical physicist. Gates was not the best undergraduate student at Harvard AND the best social organizer AND the best athlete. Instead they both looked multidimensional across a lifetime because they have moved through multiple cycles of extreme concentration.

Perhaps there is some validity to Aristotle’s argument that we cannot fully determine a person’s happiness before their later stages of life, because I’ve been trying to make the cross-section at 19 comparable to the completed biography of a 50-year-old exceptional person. So then to make age 19 consequential in the grander arc of my life, I should be certain that when I encounter something worth betting on, I am capable of going very, very deep.

That’s probably the redesign my life needs.
